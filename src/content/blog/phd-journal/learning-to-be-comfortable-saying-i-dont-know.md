For a long time, I treated "I don't know" as a confession. Research slowly changed it into a tool.

The shift was not dramatic. It was a series of small meetings where the useful sentence was not my guess—it was an honest boundary around what I had tested versus what I was assuming.

## A meeting where uncertainty helped

I was presenting early ShadoNet results to my advisor. The slides showed improving detection metrics. I had a paragraph prepared about morphology-aware learning. Midway through, I said something I would have avoided a year earlier: "I don't know yet whether the model is learning shape or stain. The metric doesn't distinguish them."

<span class="margin-note">The quality of the next question mattered more than the performance of immediate certainty.</span>

That admission did not end the conversation. It redirected it—toward sanity patches, toward evaluation slices, toward the distinction between pipeline bugs and objective failures. The meeting ended with a clearer experiment plan than any amount of confident hand-waving would have produced.

<aside class="callout callout--key-idea" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Key idea</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Not knowing is useful when it is specific: "I don't know whether X or Y explains this failure" invites collaboration. Vague uncertainty ("I don't know what's wrong") only invites anxiety.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## The thing I misunderstood

In coursework, confusion usually meant I had missed a concept. In research, confusion can mean the implementation is wrong, the baseline is stronger than expected, the idea is underdeveloped—or the field has not agreed on the right abstraction yet.

I used to treat all confusion as a personal deficit. That made me slower, not faster, because I hid the confusion until it became expensive.

<aside class="callout callout--observation" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Observation</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Turning unknowns into small experiments, reading tasks, or explicit questions for collaborators works better than pretending to know while coding.</p>
</div>
</aside>

Reading [foundation models are powerful but not magic](/blog/research-notes/foundation-models-are-powerful-but-not-magic) helped normalize this. The paper's value is partly in what it does not claim. I am trying to practice that discipline in conversations, not only in writing.

## What changed in my behavior

I started prefixing status updates with what I know, what I think, and what I have not tested. It sounds bureaucratic. It prevents a common failure mode: presenting a hypothesis as a result because the slide deck was already made.

I also started asking "what would you need to see to believe this?" instead of "does this sound good?" The second question invites politeness. The first invites science.

<aside class="callout callout--misconception" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Common misconception</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Asking for help is not a failure of independence. Research is not a performance of already knowing—it is a practice of becoming precise enough that other people can think with you.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## Open question I am sitting with

I want to experiment with a lab notebook section titled "known unknowns"—updated weekly, linked to the next action for each item. Not a todo list of tasks, but a map of what I am deliberately not claiming yet.

I still do not know how to distinguish productive uncertainty from procrastination dressed as intellectual humility. That distinction might be the slow skill I am actually learning—and I suspect it has no clean rule, only better examples over time.
