I thought the main challenge would be learning more technical material. That was true, but incomplete. The deeper challenge was learning how to behave when the problem refuses to become simple.

## A memory from the first semester

I spent an afternoon in the library with a stack of nucleus segmentation papers, convinced that if I read enough of them, the right method would announce itself. It did not. What announced itself was disagreement: different tasks, different label types, different claims about clinical relevance—often in papers with similar abstract language.

<span class="margin-note">The PhD is less like finishing a long assignment and more like learning which uncertainty deserves your attention.</span>

I left with more tabs open and less confidence. That sounds like failure in coursework terms. In research terms, it was the first day the problem felt real.

<aside class="callout callout--key-idea" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Key idea</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Reading widely without a question produces anxiety. Reading with a question—even a premature one—produces friction you can work with.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## Things I wish someone had said plainly

**Progress is not always measurable.** A good week can end with fewer answers and a sharper question. The external signs—figures, numbers, drafts—may be unchanged while the internal map moves.

**Writing is part of thinking, not a report after thinking.** Bad writing reveals bad thinking faster than more code. I learned this late; see [why writing is harder than coding](/blog/phd-journal/why-writing-is-harder-than-coding).

**Evaluation shapes the question from day one.** If you cannot describe how you would know the model learned what you claim, you probably do not understand the claim yet.

**Asking for help is not admitting defeat.** It is how research becomes precise enough for others to think with you.

<aside class="callout callout--observation" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Observation</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Keeping a research diary—assumptions before experiments, uneasy results even when metrics improve—has mattered more than any app I tried for productivity.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## What coursework did not prepare me for

In assignments, confusion usually means you missed a concept. In research, confusion can mean many things at once: wrong code, strong baseline, leaky data, underdeveloped idea—or no agreed abstraction in the field yet.

That multiplicity is exhausting until you learn to disambiguate it. [What my literature review actually looked like](/blog/phd-journal/what-my-literature-review-actually-looked-like) is the reading-side version of the same lesson.

<aside class="callout callout--misconception" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Common misconception</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Technical difficulty is only one layer in computational pathology. Gigapixel images and noisy labels are hard—but judgment under uncertainty is harder, and it is rarely listed in the syllabus.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## Open question I am sitting with

I want to write a "first-year letter" to myself every January—not advice polished for public consumption, but a private list of what felt confusing and what helped. The goal is not motivation. It is memory.

I still do not know which of these lessons transfer to someone else's PhD and which are specific to my temperament and field. I am comfortable saying that uncertainty out loud now—which might be the most useful thing on this list.
