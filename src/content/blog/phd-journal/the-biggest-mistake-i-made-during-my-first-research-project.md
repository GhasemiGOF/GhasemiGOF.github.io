My biggest early mistake was not a technical error. It was that I became loyal to a version of the problem too quickly.

## The project I held too tightly

My first serious research project started from a method I admired—a complex architecture I wanted to apply to histopathology. I spent weeks implementing before I could explain, in one paragraph, what biological question the model was supposed to answer. The paragraph kept changing depending on whether I was talking to a clinician, a machine learning colleague, or myself.

<span class="margin-note">An idea can be interesting and still not be the right handle on the problem.</span>

I treated that instability as a temporary inconvenience. It was a signal I ignored.

<aside class="callout callout--key-idea" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Key idea</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Loyalty to an early idea feels like focus from the inside and like premature closure from the outside. The fix is not cynicism—it is writing the failure mode before writing the method.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## What I should have done instead

Two practices I use now—but did not then:

**Write the failure mode first.** What would a seductive but wrong solution look like? What metric would it optimize? If I cannot answer, I do not understand the problem yet.

**Ask what result would change my mind.** Not "what result would be publishable"—what result would make me abandon the approach? If nothing would, I am not doing science; I am decorating an idea.

[Why evaluation matters more than another 1% accuracy](/blog/research-notes/why-evaluation-matters-more-than-another-1-accuracy) states this more cleanly than I understood at the time. Evaluation is not a final chapter. It shapes the question from the beginning.

<aside class="callout callout--observation" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Observation</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>I eventually pivoted—not because the code failed spectacularly, but because the evaluation I had chosen could not distinguish the story I wanted to tell from a texture shortcut. The pivot felt like failure. It was closer to clarity.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## The slow skill I am still building

Judgment means knowing when to debug for another day and when to step back. Early in the PhD, every setback looked like a reason to push harder on the same idea. Now I try to ask whether the setback is local (a bug) or structural (a wrong handle on the problem).

I am not consistently good at this. When an idea is exciting, I still under-estimate how much work honest evaluation requires.

<aside class="callout callout--misconception" role="note">
<p class="callout-label">Common misconception</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Abandoning an approach is not the same as wasting time—if you leave a record of why it stopped working. Without that record, the same mistake returns with a new architecture name.</p>
</div>
</aside>

## Open question I am sitting with

Before starting my next new project thread, I want to write a one-page "pre-mortem": how this idea could fail seductively, what evaluation would expose it, and what I would do if the first month of experiments looks promising but ambiguous.

I still do not know how early is too early to pivot. Too early and you never push through difficulty; too late and you accumulate sunk-cost loyalty. I have heuristics now, not answers—and I am trying to treat that as normal rather than as a personal failing.
